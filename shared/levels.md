# career-kit level ladder

The one ladder every career-kit workflow uses.

Rubric files never name a specific technology, tool, vendor, product, branded methodology, or certification. Levels are judged on scope and impact, not on stack, and technical fluency by the trade-offs a person made, not the tools they list. Matching specific technologies is the job of the JD match, using the JD's own wording.

Start from the person's title, then confirm it with evidence of **scope and impact**. Titles are a good default; evidence decides when the two disagree.

---

## 1. Signal dimensions

Every level is described on the same six dimensions, for both tracks. This shared vocabulary is what makes comparing two targets possible.

| Dimension | Question it answers |
|---|---|
| **Scope** | How big is the thing they own: a feature, a system, a product area, an org? |
| **Ambiguity** | Are problems handed to them, or do they find and frame the problems? |
| **Depth** | How strong is their craft: technical depth (Engineering), product or program judgment (PM-T, TPM)? |
| **Influence** | Who changes what they do because of this person: their team, other teams, the org, the industry? |
| **People** | Mentoring, hiring, growing others. Formal for managers, informal for ICs. |
| **Business impact** | Do outcomes connect to customers, revenue, cost, risk, or reliability, with evidence? |

---

## 2. IC ladder

| Dimension | Mid | Senior | Staff | Principal |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Scope** | Features and components within a team's system | A system or service end to end | Several systems or a cross-team area | A domain across an org or the company |
| **Ambiguity** | Well-defined tasks; asks good questions | Turns a loose goal into a plan, often over a half or a year | Frames problems nobody has assigned; plans roughly one to two years out | Sets multi-year technical or product direction |
| **Depth** | Solid execution with some guidance | Go-to person for their system | Sets standards others follow | Recognised authority in the domain across the company, often outside it too |
| **Influence** | Own work | Own team | Multiple teams | Org-wide; shapes leadership decisions |
| **People** | Helps peers | Mentors juniors; leads small projects | Grows and sponsors Seniors; leads cross-team efforts without authority | Grows and sponsors Staff engineers; shapes how the org works |
| **Business impact** | Delivers assigned outcomes | Measurable outcomes for their system | Outcomes across teams, tied to business goals | Outcomes that change the company's trajectory, cost base, or risk profile |

### What it looks like on a resume

| Level | Typical evidence | Common underselling |
|---|---|---|
| **Mid** | "Built X", "Implemented Y", shipped features with clear results | Lists tasks instead of outcomes |
| **Senior** | Owned a service; led a project; designed a system; on-call owner; mentoring | Writes "worked on" for things they led |
| **Staff** | Cross-team designs; technical strategy docs; migrations spanning teams; standards adopted by others | Hides cross-team work under one team's bullets; no mention of influence without authority |
| **Principal** | Org-wide architecture; multi-year roadmaps; external talks, papers, or patents; decisions that changed company direction | Reads like a strong Senior with a long tenure; no evidence of org-level decisions |

---

## 3. Manager / Leader ladder

Team sizes below are engineering figures. Product and program teams are smaller, and some engineering specialisms (for example security) often run small orgs. Each discipline's leader files set their own sizes; where they differ, the discipline file wins. Also credit the size of the wider org a leader shapes, not only their direct team. In product and program disciplines the Senior Manager and Director size ranges overlap; there, Director scope rests on structure (managers of managers, several groups) more than on headcount.

Leader-track **Scope** needs direct reports or formal accountability for a team. Leading people without reports (tech lead, acting lead) is real evidence, but it rates **Weak** for Leader Scope.

The two tracks are parallel, not ranked. Moving to management is a change of job, not a promotion, and neither track outranks the other at equivalent scope. Judge each track on its own dimensions; a move between them is a transition to be judged fresh, never an automatic step up or down.

| Dimension | Manager | Senior Manager | Director |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Scope** | One team, roughly 4 to 10 people | Several teams, or one unusually large or critical team, often including managers | An org of several teams and managers, often 25 to 100+ people |
| **Ambiguity** | Executes on a team charter | Defines charters for several teams | Defines the org's strategy and structure |
| **Depth** | Credible in the craft; makes sound technical or product calls | Judges trade-offs across teams | Judges trade-offs across the org; sets the bar for quality |
| **Influence** | Own team and close partners | Peer managers and partner orgs | Senior leadership; shapes company planning |
| **People** | Hires, coaches, gives feedback, handles performance; sponsors people into bigger roles | Manages or grows managers; builds hiring pipelines | Builds leadership benches; designs the org |
| **Business impact** | Team delivery and health | Outcomes across teams; headcount and planning | Org outcomes tied to company goals; budget ownership |

### What it looks like on a resume

| Level | Typical evidence | Common underselling |
|---|---|---|
| **Manager** | Team size; hiring numbers; delivery outcomes; retention; growing people | Bullets describe their own IC work, not the team's results |
| **Senior Manager** | Number of teams and managers; cross-team programs; planning and headcount | No numbers for team size or managers reporting to them |
| **Director** | Org size; budget; strategy; org design; executive partnership | Reads like a Senior Manager: delivery bullets, no strategy or org-shaping evidence |

## 4. Discipline notes

The ladders apply to every discipline. These are the kinds of evidence that carry the most weight in each one.

| Discipline | Strong evidence |
|---|---|
| **Software engineering** | Systems designed and owned, scale handled, performance and cost wins, quality and delivery speed |
| **SRE** | SLOs that drive decisions (error budget policies agreed with product teams), repeat incidents removed, on-call health, toil removed, capacity ahead of demand, reliability architecture. Scope is often measured by services or engineers supported |
| **Platform / Infra / DevOps** | Platform adopted by internal teams by choice rather than mandate, self-service replacing manual requests, developer productivity and delivery speed, infrastructure as code, unit cost |
| **Data engineering** | Who relies on the data, data quality and freshness, data incidents reduced, governance and access (personal data, retention, audits), cost, enabling analytics and ML teams. Data volume is context, not a level signal |
| **Security engineering** | Classes of vulnerability eliminated, secure defaults and guardrails adopted, control coverage, time to detect and fix, threat models, risk reduced; audit and compliance outcomes as supporting evidence |
| **PM-T** | Products shipped, customer problems found and solved, adoption and revenue metrics, roadmap ownership, technical trade-offs made with engineering |
| **TPM** | Cross-team programs delivered, dependencies and risks managed, technical risks caught and trade-offs decided, launch quality, process improvements, number of teams coordinated |

### SRE or Platform?
Many people do both. Decide by what the work was mainly **for**: production reliability (SLOs, incidents, on-call) is SRE; developer speed and platform adoption is Platform. When it is close, ask which one they are applying for and use that rubric.

## 5. Titles

- Use the person's title as the starting point. Senior, Staff, Principal, Manager, Senior Manager, and Director largely mean the same across tech companies.
- Check the title against the evidence in sections 2 and 3. Flag it only when the two clearly disagree, and say which way: the resume undersells the title, or the title is ahead of the evidence.
- Small companies and startups often give bigger titles for smaller scope (for example a "Director" with three reports). Judge by scope.
- "Lead" or "Tech Lead" is a role, not a level. Place the person by evidence.
- "Head of" (for example Head of Product, Head of Data) depends on company size and can mean any leader level. Judge by scope.
- "Group" titles (for example Group Product Manager) can be an IC or a manager. Check for direct reports.
- Staff and Principal titles are inconsistent in product and program roles; some companies go from Senior straight to Principal. Judge by scope.
- "DevOps engineer" covers a very wide range of scope. Judge by evidence.
- Title scope varies by region and company size: a "Director" in a small or flatter organisation, or in some markets, may have Senior Manager or Staff-level scope. Judge by scope, never by region alone.

## 6. Weighing outcomes
A number alone doesn't set the level; its reach does.

| Reach | Example | Usually signals |
|---|---|---|
| **Local** | Cut one service's build time by 15% | Mid or Senior |
| **Team or system** | Cut a product's error rate by half | Senior |
| **Cross-team** | A platform adopted by 40 teams that cut feedback time from 45 to 8 minutes | Staff |
| **Org or company** | Infrastructure cost down by a set amount a year, or a new business capability | Principal or Director |

Judge outcomes by who benefits and how widely, not by the size of the percentage.

The outside-observer test says the same thing more plainly: could you mention this outcome to someone outside the team, and would they repeat it? A Senior-level outcome comes up in a team or product review; a Staff-level outcome comes up in an engineering-leadership meeting; a Principal or Director outcome is the kind of thing said in front of company leadership or on a stage outside the company.

## 7. Principal or Director?
Both work at org scale. The difference is the kind of authority.

| | Principal (IC) | Director (Leader) |
|---|---|---|
| **Authority from** | Technical judgment: decisions, designs, and standards others adopt | Ownership: headcount, budget, org structure |
| **Typical evidence** | Org-wide architecture, technical strategy accepted by leadership, authority others defer to | Org size and managers reporting, budget, org design, leadership bench |
| **Red flag** | Org-scale claims with no decisions they personally drove | Org-scale title with no people or budget evidence |
- Do not map career-kit levels to any specific company's internal levels.

## 8. How levels are actually awarded

For the person aiming at the next level, this is how promotion committees usually think:

- **Levels are conservative.** Promotion typically lags demonstrated performance: the committee wants the next level's scope already shown, consistently, over time — not promised. That lag is risk-aversion, not a slight.
- **Sustained scope beats a heroic quarter.** A one-off Staff-sized project after a year of Senior-sized work usually reads as Senior-with-a-highlight, not Staff.
- **The two common errors mirror each other.** A bigger title for unchanged scope is the most common overshoot; genuinely grown scope under an unchanged title is the most common undersell. Evidence, not the title, decides which one a resume shows.
- **Years earn nothing by themselves.** In selection research, years of experience is among the weakest predictors of performance; scope and reach decide the level.
