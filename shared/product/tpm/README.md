# TPM rubric (Technical Program Manager)

Covers technical program managers who drive delivery of technical programs across engineering teams: planning, dependencies, risks, technical trade-offs, launches, cross-team execution, and process improvement. They are internal facing. They make sure the right work lands across many teams, on time and safely, and they are judged on what the program delivered.

## Scope and boundaries
- **In scope:** program managers whose programs are technical and whose partners are mainly engineering teams. This includes software, infrastructure, platform, security, data, and hardware programs, and release or launch programs.
- **Not PM-T.** Product Managers, Technical are a separate career-kit discipline. PM-Ts are customer facing: they decide *what* gets built and *why*, and are judged on product outcomes such as adoption and revenue. TPMs own *how* the work gets delivered across teams and are judged on delivery outcomes. A resume full of customer research, roadmap calls, and adoption metrics reads as PM-T. Judge with the files of the discipline the person is applying in; for a move between the two, see `utils/transitions/discipline-switch/`.
- **Not non-technical program or project management.** Program and project managers in operations, marketing, people, events, or business functions are out of scope, and so are delivery roles that only run a team's meetings and trackers. If a resume shows no technical content at all (no system, dependency, or trade-off named), say so and apply only the parts that still hold.

## Notes for the evaluator
- **Depth means two things here: technical fluency and program judgment.** Technical fluency is knowing the systems well enough to challenge a design, spot a technical risk before it becomes a schedule problem, and explain a trade-off to both engineers and leaders. Program judgment is sequencing, cutting scope, choosing what to escalate, and knowing when to stop or re-plan. Strong evidence shows both. A resume full of process words with no system or trade-off named is Weak on Depth.
- **TPMs do not need to write production code.** Do not reward coding for its own sake, and do not mark someone down for not coding. Former engineers often undersell their program work by writing engineering bullets; flag that as a Reframe.
- **Scope means the number and criticality of teams and programs.** Ask how many teams the program crossed, how many engineers, how many programs ran at once, and how critical the work was (revenue path, security, a hard external date). One critical program across 4 teams can outweigh three routine ones across 10.
- **Business impact is delivery outcomes.** Valid outcomes include launches delivered, dates hit, time to market cut, risk avoided (incidents, failed launches, compliance deadlines met), engineering time saved, cost saved, and quality at launch (rollbacks, defects, incidents). Many TPMs never see revenue figures; these outcomes are full credit.
- **"Delivered on time" is not enough on its own.** It becomes Strong when the bullet shows the size of the program and what the TPM did to get it there: the dependency they unblocked, the risk they caught, the scope they cut.
- **Separate the TPM's part from the engineers' work.** TPMs often write "built" or "migrated" for work engineers did. Credit outcomes when the TPM's part is clear: the plan, the decision, the risk caught, the alignment won. If it is not clear, ask: `[CONFIRM: what was your part in this?]`. Never let a rewrite claim engineering work the person did not do.
- **Process improvements count when adopted and measured.** A new planning, launch, or incident process is Strong evidence under Influence when other teams use it and something got better. A process that only exists on paper is Weak.
- **Titles vary.** Some companies go from Senior straight to Principal with no Staff title, so a "Principal TPM" may have Staff scope. Some use "Program Manager" or "Engineering Program Manager" for technical roles. Place the person by evidence, not title.
- **Leader team sizes are smaller than in engineering.** A TPM org is much smaller than the engineering org it serves. The leader files use TPM team sizes, not the engineering sizes in `levels.md`. Credit leaders for the size of the engineering org their programs cover, but judge people management on the TPMs they manage.
- **TPM managers often still own programs.** Many first-line TPM managers run a program themselves alongside their team. Do not mark them down for it. If own program work dominates and reports are few, judge the dominant track and count the other side as supporting evidence.
- **Years of experience are not a level signal.** Judge scope and impact.
- **Written communication counts heavily.** Program plans, decision documents, risk reviews, launch readiness reviews, and executive updates are evidence under Depth and Influence from Senior up.
